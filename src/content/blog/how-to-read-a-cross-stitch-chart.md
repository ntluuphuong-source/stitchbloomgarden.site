A cross stitch chart can look like a wall of tiny symbols the first time you open it. The good news is that every chart follows the same few rules, and once you know them you can read almost any pattern. This guide walks through the parts of a chart in the order you will use them.

## 1. The chart is a map of squares

Each square on the chart equals one stitch on your fabric. A symbol in the square tells you which thread color to use. Empty squares are usually left unstitched (the background fabric shows through).

If you are new to the fabric itself, read our [beginner cross stitch guide](/blog/beginner-cross-stitch-guide/) first, then come back here.

## 2. The color key (legend)

Somewhere on the pattern is a key that lists each symbol with:

- the thread brand and color number (for example DMC 310),
- the color name,
- how many strands to use,
- and often how much thread you need.

Before you start, match each symbol to a skein and write the symbol on the label or a small card so you do not mix them up.

## 3. Grid lines and the heavy lines

Charts have light grid lines for every square and heavier lines every 10 squares. Those heavier lines are there to help you count. If you need to find a spot, count in tens: it is much faster than counting single squares, and it prevents the common mistake of starting a section one square off.

## 4. Find the center

Most patterns mark the center with arrows on the edges of the chart. Follow the arrows inward until they meet at the center square. Fold your fabric in half twice to find the center on the fabric, then start there or work out from it so the design ends up properly placed. Our [Aida count guide](/blog/aida-count-finished-size/) explains how to check that your fabric is large enough for the finished size.

## 5. Types of stitches you will see

- **Full cross stitches** are the symbol squares. A cross is two diagonal stitches, with all the top stitches facing the same direction.
- **Half stitches** (one diagonal) are sometimes shown as a symbol with a diagonal line, and add shading.
- **Three-quarter and fractional stitches** can appear along curves, and are marked with a smaller or triangular symbol in the square.
- **Backstitch** appears as solid lines drawn over the chart. It is stitched last, to add outlines and fine details like whiskers or lettering.
- **French knots** are often shown as a small dot or a special symbol, and the key will tell you which thread to use.

## 6. Where to start

There is no single correct way, but three methods are popular:

1. **Start at the center** and work outward. Good for beginners because it keeps the design centered.
2. **Start at the top left** and work in rows. Good for smaller, simpler designs.
3. **Work by color** in blocks. Good for large areas of a single color, but be careful with counting.

Whichever you choose, finish each section before moving on, and complete the backstitch last.

## 7. Printed vs. digital charts

Digital PDF patterns are easy to enlarge, which helps if the symbols are small. Many stitchers print a copy and mark off finished sections with a highlighter or sticky notes. Others use a tablet or a stitching app. Pick whatever is easiest on your eyes.

## 8. A simple practice routine

If you are nervous, try this on a small piece:

1. Choose a very small design with only a few colors, such as a bookmark.
2. Sort your thread by symbol and label each skein.
3. Find the center and begin there.
4. Stitch one color at a time in small blocks.
5. Check your count every 10 squares.
6. Add the backstitch last.

## Common mistakes (and easy fixes)

- **Miscounting at the start.** Count from the center arrows and check against the heavy lines.
- **Mixing up similar colors.** Label every skein and use a good light when stitching.
- **Stitching crosses in different directions.** Pick a direction for the top stitch and keep it the same for the whole project.
- **Skipping the key.** Always check the legend before starting a new color.
