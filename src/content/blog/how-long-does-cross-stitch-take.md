"How long will it take?" is the first question most stitchers ask, and the honest answer is: it depends on you. Some people complete a hundred stitches in ten minutes, others take twice as long, and both are completely normal. The good news is that you do not need to guess. With one simple test and some arithmetic, you can estimate your own time before you buy the fabric.

We will work it out in three steps, then use the result to plan a project you want to finish by Christmas.

## Step 1: Count the stitches in your pattern

Every pattern lists its size in stitches, for example *82 x 83 stitches*. Multiply width by height to get the number of squares on the chart: 82 x 83 = 6,806.

Not every square is stitched, because patterns often have empty background. So estimate how much of the chart is filled. A design with a lot of blank space might be 40% filled, a full landscape closer to 100%. For our example, if it is about 60% filled:

6,806 x 0.60 = about 4,080 stitches

Some patterns also tell you the exact total, so check the pattern notes first. Our [Aida count guide](/blog/aida-count-finished-size/) shows how stitch counts become inches on your fabric.

## Step 2: Time yourself for 10 minutes

Speed varies with experience, fabric, the number of color changes and even how comfortable your chair is, so published averages are not much use. Do this instead:

1. Set a timer for 10 minutes.
2. Stitch normally, on the real project, with the real fabric.
3. Count the stitches you completed. A full cross counts as one stitch.
4. Multiply by 6 to get your stitches per hour.

Do it a second time on another day, because your first try may be slower while you find your rhythm. Use the average of the two.

## Step 3: Divide

Hours needed = total stitches / stitches per hour

Then divide the hours by how long you stitch each day to get the number of days.

## Example: a 3,840-stitch project

The speeds below are examples to show how the math works, not averages. Replace them with your own number from step 2.

| Your speed | Total hours | At 30 min a day | At 1 hour a day |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 stitches per hour | 38.4 hours | 77 days | 39 days |
| 200 stitches per hour | 19.2 hours | 39 days | 20 days |
| 300 stitches per hour | 12.8 hours | 26 days | 13 days |

You can see why small designs are so popular for gifts. A tiny ornament of about 30 x 30 squares, around 60% filled, is roughly 540 stitches. At 100 stitches per hour that is about 5.4 hours, or around 11 days at 30 minutes a day.

## What slows you down (and what speeds you up)

- **Many color changes.** Switching thread often takes extra time compared with stitching long runs of one color, so patterns with few colors can feel quicker.
- **Fine fabric.** Stitching on 18 or 22 count Aida takes more care than 14 count, because the holes are smaller.
- **Backstitching and details.** Thin outlines and lettering are slow but they finish a design nicely.
- **Good light and a hoop.** Being able to see the holes clearly saves time and mistakes.
- **A parking method.** Stitching a whole color in one area before moving on is faster for some stitchers than jumping around the chart.

## Plan a project before Christmas

Christmas is about seven weeks away. To work backwards:

1. Leave the last week free for finishing and wrapping. That gives you roughly six weeks of stitching time.
2. Take the days you have, for example 42, and decide how many days you will really stitch. Be realistic about busy evenings.
3. Compare your time-needed from the formula above to your available hours. If it does not fit, pick a smaller pattern.

## A few ways to stay on track

- **Stitch a little every day** rather than saving it for a free weekend. Even 20 minutes adds up.
- **Check your progress weekly.** If you are behind, shrink the project (for example, finish just the main motif) instead of rushing.
- **Keep the kit together.** A small bag with the hoop, floss, needle and scissors lets you start in seconds.

Now set your 10-minute timer and find out your own number. Happy stitching!
