Search for "cross stitch and mental health" and you will find hundreds of articles saying stitching lowers stress, boosts happiness and even changes your brain chemistry. Many of them name no study at all. We went looking for the actual research. Here is what it says, and what it does not.

The short version: **the research is encouraging, but it is mostly about related crafts and mostly based on what people report about themselves.** Almost none of it is about cross stitch alone, and none of it shows that stitching treats a mental health condition.

## A review of 25 studies on needlecraft

The widest look so far is a 2024 scoping review by Le Lagadec and colleagues in the journal *Issues in Mental Health Nursing*. They searched for research on needlecraft and mental health and included [25 studies](https://researchoutput.csu.edu.au/en/publications/healing-stitches-a-scoping-review-on-the-impact-of-needlecraft-on/) covering sewing, crochet, knitting, lace making, embroidery and quilting.

They grouped the findings into four themes:

1. **Mental well-being**, such as calm, relaxation and positive emotions.
2. **Social connection**, including friendship and belonging.
3. **Purpose and achievement**, including the pride of making and gifting.
4. **Identity, family, culture and legacy.**

The authors concluded that needlecraft has an "overwhelmingly positive effect" on mental health and well-being.

A discussion of the review on the [Creative Restoration journal club](https://creativerestoration.org/journal-club-healing-stitches/) points out why that should be read with care. It was a scoping review, which maps what exists but does not judge how good each study is. Most studies were qualitative or observational. Participants were mostly women, middle-aged or older and often already experienced crafters, so people who enjoy crafts are over-represented. Knitting was the most studied craft (8 studies), and embroidery appeared in only 2.

## A survey of more than 3,500 knitters

One of the best-known pieces of research is a survey of knitters by Riley, Corkhill and Morris, published in 2013 in the [*British Journal of Occupational Therapy*](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/43874/). More than 3,500 people responded. A researcher on the project described the main finding in an [interview with The Naked Scientists](https://www.thenakedscientists.com/node/199151): the more often people knit, the calmer and happier they said they felt.

The same interview raises the obvious caveats. It was a self-report survey of people who already knit, with no comparison group, and it was run through the network of an organization that promotes knitting. It cannot tell us whether knitting made people calmer or whether calmer people knit more often.

## What a lab measured: art making and stress hormone

For something measured in the body, there is a 2016 study by Girija Kaimal and colleagues at Drexel University, described in [a Drexel news release](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2016/June/Art_Hormone_Levels_Lower). It involved **39 adults** who spent **45 minutes** making art with markers, clay and collage. Researchers measured cortisol, a stress-related hormone, in saliva before and after.

- About **75%** of participants had lower cortisol afterwards.
- About **25%** had higher cortisol, which the researchers noted may reflect arousal or engagement rather than something bad.
- Previous art experience made no difference: nearly half the group had little experience, and "everyone seems to benefit equally," in the lead researcher's words.

This was art making, not stitching, and one session in a small group. Still, it fits the idea that you do not need to be skilled for a creative session to help you unwind.

## Do creative days feel better?

A 2018 study by Conner, DeYoung and Silvia in the *Journal of Positive Psychology* followed **658 young adults for 13 days**. People filled in a daily diary on creative activity, mood and a sense of flourishing. [On days after more creative activity than usual](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/everyday-creative-activity-as-a-path-to-flourishing/), they reported higher positive emotion and flourishing. The reverse did not hold: feeling good did not predict doing more creative activity the next day.

That direction is a hopeful sign, though it is a study of young adults, and a daily diary cannot fully prove cause and effect.

## What the research does not show

- **It does not show that cross stitch treats anxiety or depression.** Meditation programs, one of the most-studied calming activities, were found in a [2014 review of 47 trials](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1809754) to give moderate improvements in anxiety and depression, and the authors found no evidence that they were better than other active treatments. Stitching has not been tested at that level.
- **It does not show that stitching is better than other hobbies.** In the interview above, the knitting researcher herself agreed that any enjoyable activity may help.
- **It does not support the specific claims you may see online** about dopamine, serotonin or heart rate changes from stitching. We could not trace those claims to a study.

## How to get the most from stitching, in practice

These are practical suggestions rather than research findings:

- **Stitch regularly in short sessions.** The knitting survey linked more frequent knitting with feeling calmer and happier, and 20 to 30 minutes is easy to fit into a day.
- **Choose a pattern that feels calming to you.** Nature scenes, simple shapes and a few colors are friendly choices: a [calm mountain landscape](https://www.etsy.com/listing/4452769478?utm_source=stitchbloomgarden.site&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=blog) is a good example.
- **Do not aim for perfect.** The art study found skill made no difference to the stress benefit.
- **Stitch with other people sometimes.** Social connection was one of the strongest themes in the review.
- **Make something for someone.** Purpose and gifting also appeared as a theme.

## When to get help

Stitching can be a lovely part of looking after yourself, but it is not a replacement for care. If you have felt low, anxious or overwhelmed for more than a couple of weeks, or if it is getting in the way of daily life, please talk to a doctor or a mental health professional.
